Cathedrals Opening Bazaars: Firms’ Open-Source Projects and Community Participation

Job market paper. Xiaofeng Gong. This version: November 6, 2026.

The question

Large firms that file patents also publish open-source code on GitHub, which anyone can read, use and build on, and anyone with an account can report a problem or propose a change. Firm-level totals cannot show whether people take part more in the projects that overlap a firm’s patented technologies. That comparison has to be made project by project, with the code and the patents in one classification, and two gaps in the data kept them apart: open-source code carries no patent class, and company databases list no GitHub accounts.

When firms publish code in the technologies they patent, who participates, and how much do new participants contribute to the firms’ projects?

The main comparison

Repositories in the technologies where their firm was recently granted patents have 29.5 percent more new participants and 45.5 percent more contributions from them in the 24 months after their first active quarter. Contributions from everyone are also higher, and there is no clear difference in new participants’ share of contributions. These are associations, and the estimates leave open why.

Abstract

This paper studies how participation in firms’ open-source projects relates to the technologies the firms patent. I link Compustat firms to their USPTO patents and GitHub accounts and use a large language model to place each project in the Cooperative Patent Classification, the scheme patent examiners use. I document that most of these projects lie in technologies in which the firm was recently granted patents. I then compare projects first active in 2015–2022, conditional on firm-by-year and technology-by-year effects and on controls for each project’s early activity, text and language. I find that projects in the firm’s patented technologies have more new participants and more contributions from them. The difference in new participants holds for people with no earlier activity on the firm’s GitHub accounts and without the five largest firms. Contributions from everyone are also higher, and among projects with a contribution there is no clear difference in new participants’ share. According to my estimates, projects in the firm’s patented technologies have 29.5 percent more new participants in the 24 months after their first active quarter, 45.5 percent more contributions from them, and 37.6 percent more contributions from everyone.

Data and method

  • Linking firms. Crosswalks link Compustat firms to their USPTO patents and to their GitHub accounts, and each link records its evidence and the year in which the firm owns the account. The patent crosswalk covers 99.7% of the 2015–2021 patents that DISCERN links to firms headquartered in the United States.
  • Classifying code. A large language model reads each repository’s name, description, topics, programming language and README, and assigns it main groups of the Cooperative Patent Classification, the detailed classes that patent examiners assign. Its labels share at least one main group with those of the European Patent Office’s text categorizer for 76.4% of the repositories both classify, against 38.5% when the label sets are dealt out at random within the same CPC section.
  • Inside. A repository is inside when at least half of its label weight lies in main groups where its firm was granted patents in the five grant years up to its first activity. Of the 102,987 repositories first active in 2015–2022, 79.7% are inside, so the comparison is with the minority outside.
  • Participation. A contribution is an issue, a pull request, a review or a comment in GH Archive’s record of GitHub’s public events, with bots left out. A new participant has no activity on the repository through its first active quarter and makes a first contribution in the following eight quarters.
  • Estimation. Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood, with firm-by-year and main-group-by-year effects and controls for each repository’s founding-quarter activity, text and language. Standard errors are clustered by firm, and p-values are adjusted by Holm’s method for the outcomes tested together.

Findings

  • More new participants. Inside repositories have 29.5 percent more new participants in their first two years than other repositories (95 percent interval 14.6 to 46.4).
  • More contributions from both groups. Contributions from new participants are 45.5 percent higher, and contributions from everyone are 37.6 percent higher. Among repositories with a contribution, new participants’ share of contributions is 1.02 points higher inside (95 percent interval −0.52 to 2.55), so the estimates do not establish that new participants account for a larger share.
  • People new to the firm. For new participants with no earlier activity at the firm on GitHub, the difference is 27.6 percent. Without the five largest firms, the estimates for new participants and their contributions are 28.1 and 40.1 percent.
  • Published perspectives. The positive difference runs against the prediction of less participation in code tied to patents. Five contrasts for where the difference should be larger, specified before the results, give no statistically clear support for their predictions.

How far the results go

  • These are conditional associations. Project importance, audience and corporate support remain possible explanations, and the estimates leave open why.
  • Inside records shared technology classes, not whether a patent covers a repository’s code, and a grant can come after a repository’s first activity.
  • The comparison is partly between repositories of different firms.
  • Reading, downloading and running code leave no trace in the record, so the data cannot tell whether inside repositories have more users or a larger share of users who leave work on them.
  • The model read each repository’s text as it stood in 2026, after every outcome window.
  • Participants are described by their earlier activity on GitHub, and no one is matched to an employer.
